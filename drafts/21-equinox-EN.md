## The Chichén Itzá Equinox and the Kukulkán Serpent Shadow

Twice a year, in the late afternoon, the stepped corners of El Castillo throw a row of triangular shadows down the side of its northern staircase. The lit wedges between them narrow towards the bottom, where a carved serpent head waits. People call it Kukulkán descending the pyramid, and tens of thousands come to watch it on the equinox.

Here is what the brochures leave out. In 2017 the archaeoastronomers **Ivan Šprajc** and **Pedro Francisco Sánchez Nava** photographed the effect every day from **15 to 29 March** and reported that across those fifteen days "the effect changes little."

So the date on your ticket does almost none of the work. Come a week either side and you see substantially the same thing as the equinox crowd — without the crowd.

## What actually happens on the north staircase

El Castillo is a nine-tier stepped pyramid: **24 m** to the top platform, **30 m** with the temple, base **55.3 m** a side. Four stairways of **91 steps** climb it — 364 in total, 365 counting the top platform.

Each tier is stepped back at the corner. As the afternoon sun swings west, those corners cast a stack of shadows across the **alfarda**, the wide sloping balustrade edging the northern staircase. Between them the stone stays lit, and the lit sections read as triangles pointing downwards.

The usual count is **seven lit triangles and six shadows between them**. Šprajc notes that with nine tiers more triangles are geometrically possible, and that how many you see depends on the date and the light — so seven is what people traditionally report, not a fixed property of the building.

At the foot of the balustrade is a carved serpent head, one of a pair flanking the stairs, and the pattern runs towards it. That is the whole effect: light and shade sliding down a stone ramp towards a snake's head. It is genuinely good. It is also, as below, not a clock.

## The dates for 2027

The **March equinox** falls on **Saturday 20 March 2027 at 20:25 UTC**. Yucatán is on **UTC−6 all year** with no clock change, so that is **14:25 local** — a Saturday, which is convenient and a reason to expect company.

The **September equinox** is **23 September at 06:02 UTC**, or **00:02 local** — two minutes past midnight, so the visitor event is built around the daylight either side.

Notice what that does to the spring date. The instant is 14:25; the display is generally reported from around 15:00. The equinox and the effect are not the same event, and in 2027 they sit about an hour apart — a sign the two were never tied together.

## The finding that should change your plans

Šprajc and Sánchez Nava documented the display **daily from 15 to 29 March 2017** and concluded that over those fifteen days **the effect changes little**. Their wider point is blunter: "no date can be determined accurately by merely observing the play of light and shadow."

As travel advice: the window is a fortnight wide, not a day wide.

So **go about a week before the equinox, or about a week after.** Mid-March to early April gives you a display hard to distinguish from 20 March, on a normal site day, with normal hours, the whole zone open, and a fraction of the people.

Two caveats. No frame-by-frame comparison of, say, 15 March against 20 March has been published, so "hard to distinguish" is our reading of "changes little" rather than a measured equivalence. And the geometry drifts as you leave the middle of the window: a week is well inside what was observed, a month is not.

## Was the shadow designed, or is it a by-product?

Here the guidebooks and the peer-reviewed literature part company.

### Where the popular story comes from

The narrative is not ancient. It traces to photographs and a description by **Jean-Jacques Rivard** in **1969–70** and to the writing of **Luis E. Arochi** in the 1970s. Their work created both the story and the pilgrimage: the mass gatherings began in the **1970s and 1980s**, not in antiquity.

### The case against a calendar function

Šprajc and Sánchez Nava make four arguments, cumulative rather than separate.

**It is not tied to a date.** Fifteen consecutive days, very little change. Something that cannot distinguish one day from a fortnight of its neighbours is not fixing a date.

**Equinoxes were not a Maya category.** They are, in Šprajc's phrasing, not directly observable without a sophisticated method — you cannot watch one sit still on the horizon the way you can a solstice. Across **106 Maya settlements** surveyed, no orientation is reliably tied to an equinox. Mesoamerican calendars used **quarter-days**, splitting the year into four equal parts falling around **22–23 March** and **20–21 September** — two days off. Their conclusion: the equinox is a concept of Western astronomy projected uncritically onto the pre-Hispanic past.

**El Castillo's orientation points elsewhere.** The perpendicular to its northern façade is offset about **17.5° east of true north**, marking sunsets around **20 May and 24 July** and dividing the year into intervals of **65 and 300 days** — multiples of 13 and 20, the numbers that actually run Mesoamerican calendars. Nothing equinoctial in it.

**The northern staircase was always going to be the one.** It is the principal stairway, it faces the Sacred Cenote, and it carries the serpent heads. Any nine-tier pyramid throws shadows on its balustrades in the afternoon. If the effect was noticed and valued, that is more plausibly symbolic than calendrical.

### The case for intentionality

Not a one-sided file. **Anne S. Dowd**, in *Maya Architectural Hierophanies* (2015), treats the display as a genuine pre-Hispanic hierophany — a deliberate staging of light on architecture — recording **seven illuminated edges** between **16:30 and 17:30** and citing Rivard's 1970 photograph as documentation. That is a peer-reviewed author on the other side, and should be weighed as one.

### The restoration question

One further hypothesis, to be handled at arm's length. El Castillo's northern balustrades had been robbed for building stone and were **rebuilt in the 1920s with newly cut blocks**; the suggestion is that clean modern edges may have created or sharpened the effect we photograph.

We could trace this only to a commercial website citing Shavelson (1986). No peer-reviewed source turned up, and Šprajc does not make the argument. So: a named hypothesis on a weak source, not an established fact.

There is a parallel on the same staircase. The famous "quetzal chirp" — the bird-like echo a clap returns at the foot of the stairs — is disputed the same way, with some acousticians reading it as an incidental consequence of diffraction from a stepped surface that became apparent after restoration. Two celebrated effects, the same open question about how much is Maya and how much is 1920s masonry.

### What the record does not contain

No pre-Columbian text describes the shadow. No colonial account records it, Landa included. No image depicts it. The earliest description we have found is Rivard's, around 1969–70.

That is an absence of evidence, not proof nobody noticed: Mesoamerican records are fragmentary and much was destroyed. But for a supposed flagship calendrical event, the silence is at least surprising.

### Where that leaves it

Not closed, but not level either: the measured, peer-reviewed evidence runs against a calendrical function.

The defensible position is that **the effect is real and impressive, and most likely a by-product of a nine-tier pyramid whose principal staircase is decorated with serpents — possibly noticed, possibly meaningful, plausibly symbolic.** The brochure version, that the Maya built a precision equinox clock, is not defensible on current evidence.

One note, because you will see the names. **Susan Milbrath** and **Anthony Aveni** are the standard authorities on Maya astronomy, but we could not verify their positions on this particular question, so we attribute none to either.

## Conditions: when to look, and whether you will see it

The timings in the sources do not agree, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. INAH and Mexican press coverage give a broad window of roughly **15:00 to 17:00 local**; the September 2026 peak was reported at **15:51**; Dowd measured **16:30–17:30**; Šprajc notes the best light comes roughly **an hour before sunset**. How long the pattern stays genuinely crisp is put at anything from about ten minutes down to "a few minutes," while the full progression across the balustrade unfolds over something like two hours.

There is no single authoritative figure. The workable plan is to be at the north face by **15:00** and stay until the light leaves the balustrade.

Weather is the real variable, and there is no published climate record for Chichén Itzá itself; the nearest usable station is **Mérida, about 120 km away**, so treat this as regional indication rather than site data. **March** in Mérida: about **8 hours of sunshine a day**, sun for **65%** of daylight hours, around **2 rainy days**, **25 mm** of rain. **September**: **7 hours**, **57%**, **14 rainy days**, **185 mm** — squarely in the rainy season.

The effect can fail to appear at all: at the September 2026 equinox it was only glimpsed through a break in the cloud. Nobody, INAH included, can promise you this shadow — one more argument for a flexible date, since with a two-week window a cloudy Saturday is an inconvenience rather than a wasted trip.

## The crowd, and what INAH shuts

**Spring 2025: 32,872 visitors over three days. Spring 2026: 24,791. Autumn 2026: about 7,000 over two days** — September runs four to five times quieter than March. There is no official visitor cap.

In spring 2026 INAH ran a **three-day arrangement, 20–22 March**: opening at **09:00** on the Friday and **08:00** on Saturday and Sunday, with **ticket sales and entry closing at 16:00** and full evacuation by **17:30**. Autumn 2026 ran **two days, 20–21 September**.

**The 2027 arrangement has not been announced.** Do not build a trip on the 2026 pattern — check INAH's official announcement in **February 2027**.

The site also gets smaller on those days. To concentrate people at the pyramid, INAH closes the outlying groups early: the **Great Ball Court**, **Thousand Columns**, **Ossuary**, **Observatory** and **Las Monjas** take their last entry at **15:30** and are cleared at **16:00**, while **Chichén Viejo** and the **Gran Museo** close entirely. Ceremonies and rituals are not permitted. Also banned: **drones, tripods, professional camera equipment without a permit, flags, costumes, alcohol and e-cigarettes** — worth weighing if photographing the shadow properly is the point of the visit.

Put plainly: on equinox weekend you pay the same money for a smaller site, a shorter day, more restrictions and twenty-odd thousand people. A week earlier you get the whole zone, ordinary hours and the same shadow.

## Alternatives across Yucatán

**Chichén Itzá on a different day** is the main recommendation here: mid-March to early April, same effect, ordinary site.

**Dzibilchaltún, Temple of the Seven Dolls.** Sunrise appears in the doorways around **06:00**, and INAH presents it as an equinox phenomenon. Šprajc's measurements complicate that: the axis is offset by only about a degree and marks the **quarter-days**, not the equinox, and the sun shows in the doorways for several mornings running. There is also a practical problem — **INAH did not open Dzibilchaltún for the spring equinox in 2026**, having no budget for a 04:00 opening. Nothing is confirmed for 2027.

**Oxkintok.** Sunrise through an arch at around **06:00**, and far quieter.

**Mayapán.** A comparable serpent-shadow effect, but around the **winter solstice** — a different trip.

**Ek Balam, Uxmal, Izamal, Kabah** are recommended in the press as places to spread the equinox crowd. All are worth visiting, but we found **no confirmed equinox alignment** at any, so treat those suggestions as crowd management rather than astronomy.

## The alignments that are actually measured

**Casa Colorada**: sunrises on **12 February and 30 October**, an interval of **260 days** — the length of the tzolk'in count.

**Las Monjas**: sunsets on **11 April and 1 September**, **143 days** apart, which is 11 × 13.

**El Caracol**: the foundational study is Aveni, Gibbs and Hartung in *Science* (1975), reading the stairway as oriented to the northern extreme of Venus. Note that Šprajc excluded El Caracol from his own survey — a round building does not give you an axis you can measure reliably.

**Platform of Venus**: despite the name, we found **no measured astronomical alignment**, so we are not claiming one.

## Frequently asked questions

**When is the Chichén Itzá equinox in 2027?**

The display runs roughly 15:00–17:00, so on the spring date the instant and the effect sit about an hour apart.

**Do I have to go on the equinox to see the serpent shadow?**
No, and this is the most useful thing to know. Šprajc and Sánchez Nava observed it daily from 15 to 29 March 2017 and found it changes little across those fifteen days. Mid-March to early April shows substantially the same display on an ordinary site day.

**What time does the shadow appear on El Castillo?**

**Did the Maya build El Castillo as an equinox marker?**
Probably not as a calendrical device. Surveys of 106 Maya settlements found no orientation reliably tied to an equinox; Mesoamerican calendars used quarter-days around 22–23 March and 20–21 September; and El Castillo's 17.5° offset marks sunsets on 20 May and 24 July, intervals of 65 and 300 days. Anne S. Dowd (2015) argues the other way, treating it as a genuine pre-Hispanic hierophany, so the question is not closed. The effect is real; the precision-clock version is not supported.
