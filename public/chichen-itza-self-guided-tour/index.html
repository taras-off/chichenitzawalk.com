The entrance moved. Most route descriptions have not caught up

For decades you entered from the west, through the old Parador Turístico, and arrived almost immediately at El Castillo. The Sacred Cenote was a there-and-back detour from the pyramid, or skipped.

That is no longer how it works.

The new CATVI visitor centre stands roughly 900 metres north of El Castillo, on federal highway 180. Yucatán's tourism secretary, Darío Flota Ocampo, has described the new sequence plainly: the cenote first, then the Castillo. You now reach the Sacred Cenote before you reach the pyramid.

The old western Parador Turístico was taken out of service in June 2026. There is one entrance now, through CATVI, and INAH has given no indication it plans to change that arrangement back.

Be careful how much you build on this, because the detail is thin. Not documented anywhere we can find: the exact line the internal path takes from CATVI to the monuments, its surface, how much of it is shaded, whether the Sacred Cenote is still a dead-end spur off the causeway or now sits on the way in, and whether any shuttle runs.

Official distance figures do not agree with each other either:

  • 900 m — Yucatán state government, CATVI to El Castillo
  • 800 m — from the ticket windows to the Sacred Cenote
  • 2 km — CATVI to the archaeological zone

So plan for something between 800 m and 2 km each way, on foot, in the open. No official walking time has been published. Thirty to sixty minutes for the round trip, on top of your time among the monuments, is a sane allowance rather than a measured one.

How the site is laid out — and why the order matters at all

If the whole archaeological zone were one architectural period, the order you walked it in would be a matter of taste. It is not, and that is the one solid reason to think about sequence.

The core covers at least 5 km². INAH counts six groups of monuments: the Gran Nivelación (the main plaza), the Grupo de las Mil Columnas, the Grupo del Osario, the Grupo de la Casa Colorada, the Grupo de Las Monjas and Chichén Viejo.

For a walking visit, they collapse into two:

The north is Toltec. El Castillo, the Great Ball Court, the Tzompantli, the Temple of the Warriors, the Thousand Columns. Broad plazas, colonnades, feathered serpents, military iconography. This is the Chichén Itzá of the postcards, and the part every group is taken to.

The south is older and Maya, in the Puuc style. El Caracol, Las Monjas, La Iglesia, the Red House. Smaller, denser, heavily ornamented, laid out without the parade-ground scale of the north.

The architectural periods are sorted geographically. Walk the site as one loop and you also walk it roughly in chronological reverse. That is the whole argument for having an order at all, and it is why "just wander" leaves most people with a strong memory of one pyramid and a vague blur around it.

Distances between the northern monuments are short. El Castillo to the Great Ball Court is about 150 m; to the Temple of the Warriors about 120 m. Sacbe No. 1, the ancient paved causeway from El Castillo to the Sacred Cenote, is 270 m long and averages 9 m wide — some sources give 300 m.

A route that works, and why

Nobody has published data on how groups distribute themselves inside the zone once they are through the gate. What is documented is the entrance: INAH's own load figures, reported via Proceso, put the pressure on the entry between 10:00 and 14:00. Coaches begin arriving around 10:00–11:00 and groups start leaving between 14:00 and 15:00. In 2024 the site took 2.2 million visitors.

So what follows is reasoning from the entrance data, not a documented crowd map. Treat it as a working plan.

If you arrive at opening. You have a clear run before the coaches, and the main plaza is what they all head for. Spend the first hour on the Gran Nivelación — El Castillo, the ball court, the platforms, the Temple of the Warriors, the colonnades — because that is the hour nobody else is there. Then, as the buses unload, move south into the Puuc group, where the group circuits spend least time.

If you arrive mid-morning, between 10:00 and noon. You are arriving inside the peak. Inverting the plan costs you nothing: go south first — El Caracol, Las Monjas, the Casa Colorada group — and leave the main plaza until after 14:00, when the coach groups start moving back towards their buses. You will see the same monuments; you will see them with fewer people in front of them.

If you arrive after 15:00. Roughly two hours before the site is cleared, and the best light of the day. Do the main plaza and the causeway and accept that the southern group may not fit — last tickets go at 16:00 and staff begin clearing from around 17:00.

The one rule that survives all three: walk against the flow. The groups are concentrated in the north, in the middle of the day. That is a fact about the entrance data and the shape of coach itineraries, and it is the only crowd-avoidance advice on this page that does not require guessing.

How long you actually need

Sources converge on 2 to 4 hours inside the archaeological zone, and the figure quoted most often is 3 hours — the six groups at an unhurried pace, with stops. Add 30 to 60 minutes for the approach from CATVI and back; no official transit time exists, so that is an allowance rather than a schedule. A guided tour of the same ground runs 90 minutes to 2 hours, and it is faster because it is selective, not because guides walk fast.

In practice: two hours buys the main plaza and the causeway and nothing else; three covers both architectural groups, which with the approach makes a four-hour visit; four or more adds the Osario and Cenote Xtoloc. In the March-to-September heat, build in time to sit in shade — that is not optional.

What there is to see — and what you cannot do

Start with the constraint, because it changes expectations more than anything else: you cannot climb anything, and you cannot go inside anything.

Climbing El Castillo has been prohibited since 2006, following a fatal fall by an 80-year-old American visitor in January of that year. The Temple of the Warriors is closed to climbing. The inner temple inside El Castillo — the one with the chac-mool and the jaguar throne — has been closed since 2006 as well.

Everything is seen from ground level, from behind a rope. That is fine if you know it in advance and a real disappointment if you do not.

What rewards attention instead:

  • The ruins were painted, predominantly red. The bare grey limestone you are looking at is a stripped surface, not the original one.
  • The columns of the Temple of the Warriors are carved figures of warriors, each one different. From the rope line they read as a grid of plain posts; the carving only resolves close up.
  • The long-snouted masks all over the southern buildings are Chaac, the rain god.
  • El Osario is a smaller version of El Castillo — same stepped form, same arrangement. This is why it ends up in so many photo albums labelled as the main pyramid.
  • The paths through the central area are real sacbeob — ancient paved causeways, not modern gravel. Sacbe No. 1 is the 270-metre one to the Sacred Cenote.

The Sacred Cenote sits at the end of that causeway, 270–300 m from El Castillo: about 60 m across, with a drop of roughly 27 m to the water. There is a viewing platform only — no descending, no swimming.

Easy to walk past

Named here so you at least know they exist: the entire southern Puuc group, the Osario, Cenote Xtoloc and the Red House. All four are inside your ticket and within the standard walking loop, and all four are routinely skipped by visitors who came for one pyramid and left after photographing it.

The two acoustic checks worth doing

This is the one section where standing in a particular spot is the point.

The Great Ball Court. The court is 168 × 70 m, walls about 8 m high. Acoustician David Lubman measured a waveguide effect: parallel walls 36 m apart and 8.5 m high producing an 83-metre guide, with a handclap echo running about 2.5 seconds. Lubman's view is that the effect is most likely original rather than a by-product of restoration.

The famous claim is that a voice carries ~140 m between the temples at opposite ends of the court. You cannot test that, because both end temples are roped off. Plenty of blog posts imply you can. What you can test, standing inside the court, is the rolling handclap echo.

El Castillo. Clap at the foot of the staircase and the return is not a flat echo but a descending chirp, widely compared to the call of a quetzal. David Lubman described it first, in 1998; Nico Declercq and co-authors published the model in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America in 2004 — the staircase acts as an acoustic filter.

Whether it was intended is an open argument, and the two people who did the measuring disagree. Declercq is sceptical — the builders were "unlikely to have predicted the exact sound," and he has found comparable effects at other ritual sites. Lubman leans towards deliberate design. Both positions come from people who took instruments to the staircase, so we will not settle it for you.

The names that trip everyone up

Half the confusion in Chichén Itzá trip reports comes from the names. Almost none of them mean what they say.

  • El Castillo = the Temple of Kukulkán. Same building, two names.
  • El Osario, also called the Temple of the High Priest, is a smaller copy of El Castillo's form — which is why it is so often photographed and posted as the main pyramid.
  • Las Monjas is not a convent. It is a palace complex; the name is colonial.
  • La Iglesia is not a church. It is a temple of Chaac.
  • El Caracol means "the snail" — named for the spiral staircase inside, not for anything astronomical about its shape.
  • The Group of a Thousand Columns has roughly two hundred columns.
  • The Tzompantli is the skull platform. It gets mixed up with the Platform of Venus, which is a different structure.
  • Three different cenotes, constantly confused. The Sacred Cenote is inside the archaeological zone and you may only look at it. Cenote Xtoloc is also inside, and was the settlement's water source. Ik Kil is a few kilometres outside the park — that is the one people swim in.

Chichén Viejo: the part you have to arrange

Chichén Viejo — the Grupo de la Serie Inicial — opened in September 2023 and is included in the standard ticket. It is the least-visited part of the site by a wide margin, because access is capped and escorted.

The shape of it: a 1.5 km trail, 4 m wide, about 2.5 hours, escorted throughout by INAH staff, taking in 25 structures across two plazas. Slots have been published as 09:00 and 12:00, 50 people per slot, 100 per day. On days, INAH said Friday and Saturday in 2023; sources from 2025–2026 say Thursday, Friday and Saturday.

Here is the problem. Every source we can find places the start of that trail at the former Parador Turístico — the entrance taken out of service in June 2026. Where it begins now is not documented anywhere.

So do not plan a day around the times above. Call 985 851 0137 or email visitas.chichenitza@inah.gob.mx and ask for the current days, slots and meeting point. Two minutes, and it saves a wasted morning.

The practical checklist

Hours — and a discrepancy worth knowing about. Gates open at 08:00, the last ticket goes at 16:00, and staff clear the zone from around 17:00. Note that INAH's own web pages still say 08:00–16:00 with last entry at 15:00, while INAH's bulletins and every commercial source in 2026 say entry until 16:00. The official pages are behind the official policy — so plan to the later hours, but do not stake a long drive on arriving at 15:30 without checking first.

The ticket. 697 MXN for a foreign adult — the Boleto Único, in force since 27 March 2026, one payment at one window inside CATVI. Mexican nationals pay 310 MXN. Yucatán residents enter free through the end of 2026. Foreign children aged 3–12 pay around 105 MXN and under-3s are free; Mexican children under 13 go free. Sunday is free for Mexican nationals and residents — which makes Sunday the busiest day of the week. If you have any flexibility, use it.

The museum. The Gran Museo de Chichén Itzá is inside CATVI: 3,400 m², 14 thematic axes, over 500 objects, free entry, open since February 2024. Whether the walk-in route obliges you to pass through it is not stated anywhere, and no figure has been published for how much time it adds. Allow some; do not build a tight schedule around a number nobody has given.

Water. There are no drinking fountains inside the archaeological zone, and water is sold on site at roughly two to three times normal prices. Bring more than you think you need.

Food. Not sold inside the archaeological zone — only on the entrance plaza.

Toilets. INAH lists them among site services and CATVI has expanded facilities, but sources disagree on whether there are any out among the monuments. Use the ones at the entrance.

Lockers. 30–40 MXN — no official price is published.

Parking. 80–120 MXN; CATVI has 517 spaces. There is an ATM.

What is not allowed in. Drones without INAH permission. Tripods. Detachable camera lenses — stricter than most guides report, but that is what INAH's own list says. Professional shoots by permit only. Also prohibited: flammables, cigarettes including e-cigarettes, costumes, banners, flags, alcohol, drugs, bladed weapons and firearms. Ceremonies are not allowed.

Backpacks are temporarily banned, a measure introduced in spring 2026 after an incident at Teotihuacán. No end date has been announced. Check before you travel — this one changes.

The vendors. A 2025 census counted 666 artisans working inside the archaeological zone. After the conflict of May 2026 they were relocated to the stretch between the new CATVI entrance and the Sacred Cenote — meaning they now line the path in. Nobody was removed; no new sellers are being admitted. Reviews consistently describe them as persistent.

There are no reliable published norms on bargaining. What follows is an observation from our own knowledge base rather than a rule: the first price asked is usually two to three times what the piece eventually sells for, and the same items on the quieter side alleys often go for half what they cost on the main path.

Frequently asked questions

Can you visit Chichén Itzá without a guide?

Yes. There is no compulsory tour and no fixed route — you buy a ticket at CATVI and walk. Most visitors do it this way; an audio guide or a guide hired at the entrance are optional additions.

How long does a self-guided tour of Chichén Itzá take?

Sources put it at 2 to 4 hours inside the archaeological zone, with 3 hours the most common answer, plus 30 to 60 minutes for the walk from the visitor centre and back. A guided tour of the same ground runs 90 minutes to 2 hours because it is selective.

How far do you walk at Chichén Itzá?

Between 3 and 5 km inside the main zone — Yucatán Today says 4 km minimum — plus the approach from the visitor centre in both directions. The core covers at least 5 km².

Where is the entrance to Chichén Itzá in 2026?

Through the new CATVI visitor centre on highway 180, roughly 900 m north of El Castillo. The old western Parador Turístico was taken out of service in June 2026, and the route now reaches the Sacred Cenote before the pyramid — the reverse of what most guidebooks describe.

What is the best order to walk Chichén Itzá?

The northern monuments are Toltec and the southern ones older Maya in Puuc style, so one loop walks you through the architectural periods in order. Arriving at opening, do the main plaza first, before the coaches. Arriving between 10:00 and noon, start in the south and leave the main plaza until after 14:00. How groups distribute inside the site is not documented, so treat that as reasoning rather than a rule.

Can you climb El Castillo?

No. Climbing has been prohibited since 2006, after a fatal fall. The Temple of the Warriors is closed to climbing too, and the inner temple inside El Castillo has been closed since the same year. Everything is viewed from ground level, from behind a rope.

What time of day is Chichén Itzá least crowded?

Before 10:00 and after 15:00. INAH's own figures put peak pressure on the entrance between 10:00 and 14:00; coaches begin arriving around 10:00–11:00 and groups leave from 14:00. Avoid Sunday if you can — entry is free for Mexican nationals and residents, which makes it the busiest day of the week.
