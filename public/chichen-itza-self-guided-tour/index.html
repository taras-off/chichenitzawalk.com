The legend of the virgins, and what was really in the well

If you have heard one thing about Chichén Itzá, it is probably this: that young virgins were thrown into the Sacred Cenote. It is a durable story, and almost entirely wrong — which makes it worth taking apart before you get there.

Start with who did not say it. Diego de Landa, writing around 1566 and the source everyone cites, does not mention virgins at all. He writes that the Maya threw in live men — and in another passage that the lords of Maní took "slaves, male and female" to cast into the well of Chichén Itzá. No maidens anywhere. The version with the beautiful young women comes from much later, and mostly from one man: Edward Thompson, who dredged the cenote from 1904 and then told the story in a popular book in 1926. He sorted the skulls by eye, decided most were girls of fourteen to twenty, and the image went around the world.

Then the bones were actually examined. In 1940 the physical anthropologist Earnest Hooton went through the material and found something much duller and much more interesting: roughly equal numbers of male and female remains, and half of them children. Every re-analysis since has pointed the same way. By the counts of later researchers, between half and two-thirds of the people in that well were under eighteen, with the heaviest concentration between six and twelve. And isotopes in their teeth say they came from all over Mesoamerica — the Gulf coast, central Mexico, Central America — not from the town next door.

So the real story is not a doomed local beauty. It is a well that drew offerings and people from across a continent for six hundred years, and that still held gold, jade, copal, carved wood and textiles when it was dredged — because the water kept out the air. Taking a legend apart is more fun than repeating it, and there are 16 of these on the walk.

Worth knowing before you walk

Start wherever you like. The route has an order, but the app does not enforce it — the map shows where each story belongs and you start it yourself.

Closed, comfortable shoes and something on your head. It is three to five kilometres over gravel and uneven stone, and the approach from CATVI and back is about two of them. A hat, water and sunscreen matter here.

Go early. Gates open at 08:00 and the coaches arrive from 10:00. The hottest hours run from midday into early afternoon, and there is no reliable shade on the esplanade.

The ticket is separate. The audio guide does not include admission. For a foreign adult that is 697 pesos at the CATVI windows; you can also buy ahead, and our tickets guide explains what the price covers.

Frequently asked questions

Can you visit Chichén Itzá without a tour?

Yes. You buy the 697-peso ticket at the CATVI windows and walk in. Nothing inside the zone requires a guide, and no part of the route is restricted to groups. The only thing you lose by going alone is the explanation — which is what the audio guide is for.

How many stories are there and how long does the walk take?

Sixteen audio stories, about an hour and a half if you walk it straight through. Most people take longer, because you can stop whenever you like and the app waits for you.

Do I need an internet connection?

No. Download the tour before you travel and everything — audio, map and route — works offline. That is deliberate: the zone is large and you should not be hunting for a signal in the heat.

Is the Chichén Itzá entry ticket included?

No. The audio guide is €9.99 and the admission is 697 pesos for a foreign adult, paid separately at the CATVI windows. We say so plainly because some listings do not.

Can I start somewhere other than the beginning?

Yes, anywhere. The map shows each story at its own spot, so you can join the route wherever you happen to be standing and take the rest in any order.

What languages is it in?

Eight: English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Polish and Russian. Everyone in your group listens in their own language on their own phone.

Is it suitable for children?

It works well for children old enough to walk three to five kilometres in heat and wear headphones. The stories are short, which helps. The site itself is harder work than the guide is.
