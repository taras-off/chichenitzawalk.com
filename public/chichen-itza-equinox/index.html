The Chichén Itzá Equinox and the Kukulkán Serpent Shadow

What the light on El Castillo's staircase actually is, why two archaeoastronomical studies agree it is not a calendar, and what the record does not contain.

By Eugene · updated 22 October 2026 · 11 min read

Late on a spring afternoon the stepped corners of El Castillo throw a row of shadows down the side of its northern staircase. The lit stone between them reads as triangles pointing downwards, and at the foot of the balustrade a carved serpent head waits. People call it Kukulkán descending the pyramid, and tens of thousands come to watch it on the equinox.

This page is about what that effect is and whether the Maya built it on purpose. It is a better question than the one most pages answer, and the honest reply is more interesting than the brochure. For which dates to pick and how bad the crowds get, our page on when to visit has the numbers.

What actually happens on the north staircase

El Castillo is a nine-tier stepped pyramid, about 30 m tall with the temple on top and about 55 m a side at the base. Four stairways of 91 steps climb it — the count comes from Diego de Landa, writing around 1566. Multiply by four and you get 364, add the top platform and you get 365, which is where the famous calendar claim comes from. INAH does not publish a total, so treat 365 as arithmetic rather than as a measured fact.

Each tier is stepped back at the corner. As the afternoon sun swings west, those corners cast a stack of shadows across the western balustrade of the northern staircase — the one INAH calls the north-western balustrade. Between the shadows the stone stays lit, and the lit sections read as triangles pointing down the slope.

The usual count is seven lit triangles. Šprajc notes that with nine tiers more are geometrically possible, and that how many you see depends on the date and the light — so seven is what people traditionally report, not a fixed property of the building.

At the foot of the balustrade is a carved serpent head, one of a pair flanking the stairs, and the pattern runs towards it. That is the whole effect: light and shade sliding down a stone ramp towards a snake's head. It is genuinely good. It is also, on the evidence below, not a clock.

Two studies, and neither of them finds a calendar

The useful thing about this question is that it has been measured twice, from different directions, and both results point the same way.

In 2017, the archaeoastronomers Ivan Šprajc and Pedro Francisco Sánchez Nava photographed the effect every day from 15 to 29 March and reported that across those fifteen days "the effect changes little."

In 2026, INAH presented work by four archaeoastronomers in Arqueología Mexicana No. 197 giving a full annual calendar of the light on the balustrade. Its finding for March is that the seven triangles forming the serpent's complete body appear from 15 to 25 March, and that what changes on the 15th is not the count but the duration: before it the triangles "disappear before sunset", and from the 15th the seven "will remain into the sunsets".

Read them together and the conclusion is the same from both ends. A phenomenon that looks substantially the same for eleven days, and barely changes across fifteen, cannot be used to identify one day. Whatever the builders were doing, they were not marking an equinox with it.

For the visitor that is good news, and it is the practical half of this page: the shadow is not an appointment. Our timing page sets out which days inside that window are quiet and which are not.

Was the shadow designed, or is it a by-product?

Here the guidebooks and the peer-reviewed literature part company.

The story is not ancient. It traces to photographs and a description by Jean-Jacques Rivard in 1969–70 and to the writing of Luis E. Arochi in the 1970s. Their work created both the narrative and the pilgrimage: the mass gatherings began in the 1970s and 1980s, not in antiquity.

Šprajc and Sánchez Nava make four arguments against a calendrical function, cumulative rather than separate.

It is not tied to a date. Fifteen consecutive days, very little change. Something that cannot distinguish one day from a fortnight of its neighbours is not fixing a date.

Equinoxes were not a Maya category. They are, in Šprajc's phrasing, not directly observable without a sophisticated method — you cannot watch one sit still on the horizon the way you can a solstice. Across 106 Maya settlements surveyed, no orientation is reliably tied to an equinox. Mesoamerican calendars used quarter-days, splitting the year into four equal parts falling around 22–23 March and 20–21 September — two days off. Their conclusion is that the equinox is a concept of Western astronomy projected uncritically onto the pre-Hispanic past.

El Castillo's orientation points elsewhere. The perpendicular to its northern façade is offset about 17.5° east of true north, marking sunsets around 20 May and 24 July and dividing the year into intervals of 65 and 300 days — multiples of 13 and 20, the numbers that actually run Mesoamerican calendars. Nothing equinoctial in it.

The northern staircase was always going to be the one. It is the principal stairway, it faces the Sacred Cenote, and it carries the serpent heads. Any nine-tier pyramid throws shadows on its balustrades in the afternoon. If the effect was noticed and valued, that is more plausibly symbolic than calendrical.

It is not a one-sided file. Anne S. Dowd, in Maya Architectural Hierophanies (2015), treats the display as a genuine pre-Hispanic hierophany — a deliberate staging of light on architecture — recording seven illuminated edges between 16:30 and 17:30 — a narrower window inside the usual one — and citing Rivard's 1970 photograph as documentation. That is a peer-reviewed author on the other side, and it should be weighed as one.

The restoration question, handled at arm's length

One further hypothesis, which we are including because you will meet it and because it deserves to be labelled properly.

El Castillo's northern balustrades had been robbed for building stone and were rebuilt in the 1920s with newly cut blocks. The suggestion is that clean modern edges may have created or sharpened the effect we photograph today.

We could trace this only to a commercial website citing Shavelson (1986). No peer-reviewed source turned up, and Šprajc does not make the argument. So: a named hypothesis resting on a weak source, not an established fact.

There is a parallel on the same staircase. The famous "quetzal chirp" — the bird-like echo a clap returns at the foot of the stairs — is disputed the same way, with some acousticians reading it as an incidental consequence of diffraction from a stepped surface that became apparent after restoration. Two celebrated effects, the same open question about how much is Maya and how much is 1920s masonry.

What the record does not contain

No pre-Columbian text describes the shadow. No colonial account records it, Landa included. No image depicts it. The earliest description we have found is Rivard's, around 1969–70.

That is an absence of evidence, not proof that nobody noticed: Mesoamerican records are fragmentary and much was destroyed. But for a supposed flagship calendrical event, the silence is at least surprising.

Where that leaves it. Not closed, but not level either: the measured, peer-reviewed evidence runs against a calendrical function. The defensible position is that the effect is real and impressive, and most likely a by-product of a nine-tier pyramid whose principal staircase is decorated with serpents — possibly noticed, possibly meaningful, plausibly symbolic. The brochure version, that the Maya built a precision equinox clock, is not defensible on current evidence.

One note, because you will see the names. Susan Milbrath and Anthony Aveni are the standard authorities on Maya astronomy, but we could not verify their positions on this particular question, so we attribute none to either.

The alignments that are actually measured

If you want Maya astronomy at Chichén Itzá, it is on the site — just not on the famous staircase.

Those are the numbers that behave like a calendar: 260, 143, 65 and 300, all built out of 13 and 20. The equinox shadow is not among them.

Watching it: what the afternoon actually requires

It needs direct sun, roughly between 15:00 and 17:00. That is the whole dependency, and it does fail: at the spring equinox of 2023 a frontal system covered the sky and the serpent did not form at all, in front of thousands of people. In 2026 cloud nearly took it and the descent appeared late.

You will be looking from further back than old photographs suggest. INAH widened the cordon around El Castillo in April 2025, to discourage unauthorised access to the base and to give the custodians a clearer line of sight.

The hours are disputed, and that matters in the afternoon. INAH's own zone page, updated 6 October 2026, gives 08:00–17:00 with last access at 16:00 and clearing from 16:30; INAH Lugares gives 08:00–16:00 with last access at 15:00. Both are official. If you are planning to be on site at 16:30 for the light, you are planning against the stricter of two official timetables. Be through the gate before 15:00 — that satisfies both pages, and the shadow has not started yet. Check with the zone on 985 851 0137 before you build a day around it.

On the equinox weekend itself INAH changes the arrangements and publishes them about a week ahead. In 2026 the gates opened at 09:00 on the Friday, ticket sales stopped at 16:00 and several monuments closed at 15:30. Chichén Viejo was shut for the weekend, and INAH's zone page now lists it as temporarily closed altogether.

The symbolism is on the pyramid every afternoon of the year

Strip the equinox out and the better story remains. El Castillo has nine tiers, four stairways of 91 steps, a principal staircase pointed at the Sacred Cenote and flanked by serpent heads, and an orientation set 17.5° east of north for reasons that have nothing to do with equinoxes. All of it is visible on a wet Tuesday in August. The shadow is one afternoon's consequence of a design that is legible all year.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Chichén Itzá equinox serpent?

A row of triangular shadows cast by the stepped corners of El Castillo down the balustrade of its northern staircase on spring afternoons, running towards a carved serpent head at the foot of the stairs. The lit stone between the shadows reads as seven triangles, and the whole figure is read as Kukulkán descending the pyramid.

Did the Maya design the serpent shadow deliberately?

Probably not as a calendar. Šprajc and Sánchez Nava argue it cannot fix a date, that equinoxes were not a Maya category, and that El Castillo's orientation is about 17.5° east of north and marks sunsets around 20 May and 24 July instead. Anne S. Dowd, in a 2015 monograph, does treat it as a deliberate hierophany, so the file is not one-sided — but the measured evidence runs against a calendrical function.

Do you have to go on the equinox to see it?

No, and that is the most useful thing on this page. One study photographed the effect daily from 15 to 29 March and found it changes little; another, presented by INAH in 2026, puts the complete seven-triangle figure between 15 and 25 March. Either way the window is a fortnight, not a day.

When was the serpent shadow first recorded?

Around 1969–70, in photographs and a description by Jean-Jacques Rivard, popularised by Luis E. Arochi in the 1970s. No pre-Columbian text or image is known to describe it, and no colonial account records it, Landa included. That is an absence of evidence rather than proof, but it is a surprising silence for a supposed flagship event.

Did the 1920s restoration create the effect?

Unproven. El Castillo's northern balustrades were rebuilt in the 1920s with newly cut blocks, and the suggestion is that clean modern edges sharpened or created the effect. We could trace that only to a commercial website citing Shavelson (1986); no peer-reviewed source supports it and Šprajc does not make the argument. Treat it as a named hypothesis on a weak source.

What time of day does the shadow appear?

Roughly between 15:00 and 17:00, and it needs direct sun. It does fail: at the spring equinox of 2023 a frontal system covered the sky and the figure did not form at all. Note also that the two official INAH pages disagree on the last entry, giving 16:00 and 15:00, so a late afternoon on site is not something to assume.

When is the spring equinox at Chichén Itzá in 2027?

Saturday 20 March 2027, at 14:25 local time. INAH normally runs its equinox operation across the Friday to Sunday around the date and publishes the arrangements about a week ahead, so expect modified hours rather than the usual timetable.

Is there any real Maya astronomy at Chichén Itzá?

Yes, and it is measured. The Casa Colorada marks sunrises 260 days apart, the length of the tzolk'in count. Las Monjas marks sunsets 143 days apart, which is 11 × 13. El Caracol was read by Aveni, Gibbs and Hartung in Science in 1975 as oriented to the northern extreme of Venus. Those intervals are built from 13 and 20, the numbers Mesoamerican calendars actually use.

Does El Castillo really have 365 steps?

Diego de Landa, writing around 1566, records 91 steps on each of the four stairways. Four times 91 is 364, and adding the top platform gives 365. INAH does not publish a total, so the famous figure is arithmetic on a sixteenth-century count rather than a measured fact.
