Best Time to Visit Chichén Itzá: Weather, Crowds and the Equinox

Real monthly visitor numbers, the weather month by month, and the eleven-day window when the equinox serpent appears without the equinox crowd.

What this actually tells you. September and October are not slightly quieter — they are 35 to 45 per cent below a peak month, two years running. May and June are the second-emptiest stretch. And July, which sits in the middle of the rainy season, was the busiest month of 2025, because Mexican school holidays beat weather every time.

November is the interesting one, and the detail matters. In 2024 it ran 20 per cent below January; in 2025, with a softer January, it ran under 4 per cent below. So November is not reliably emptier than midwinter. What it reliably is: drier than the autumn, cooler than the spring, and never the peak. That is a different claim, and it is the one the data supports.

A note on the totals. Chichén Itzá took about 2.25 million visitors in 2024 and 2.18 million in 2025, and 2026 is running down: January to August was 13 per cent below the same stretch of 2025. Three different official figures circulate for 2024 — the department revised its own — so treat annual totals as "about", and the monthly shape, consistent across both years, as solid.

The shape is simple. December, January and February are the only months whose average high stays near 30 °C. March, April and May climb hard: April 2026 was the fourth-hottest April on record in Mérida, peaking at 42.5 °C, with 43 °C in inland municipalities and a state record of 49 °C from 2015. June to August look cooler on paper — 34.5 °C — but humidity sits at 75 per cent, so they are not.

And remember what the site is. Almost no shade, a 900-metre walk from the entrance before you reach the first monument, and three to five kilometres of walking once you are in.

Rain, and what it actually does to a visit

The wet season runs roughly 24 May to 23 October. The Atlantic hurricane season is 1 June to 30 November, with the climatological peak on 10 September — which is also the wettest month at the site.

Two things worth separating.

Everyday rain is survivable. Yucatán rain in season is usually convective and afternoon-heavy: CONAGUA's regional forecasts routinely say the potential for storms builds in the afternoon, and "lluvias vespertinas" is the standard phrase. Arrive at opening and a wet-season day often gives you a dry morning and a soaked bus ride home. Nobody publishes the share of rainfall that falls after midday, so treat that as the pattern rather than a rule.

Weather closures happen, rarely. The zone reopened on 29 October 2020 after a tropical storm and two hurricanes, and INAH closed Chichén Itzá, Uxmal and Dzibilchaltún on 2 June 2026 during heavy rain, calling it a precaution. There is no published statistic on how often this happens, and no figure for how often a hurricane reaches inland Yucatán — the site sits about 120 km from the coast. Storms do cross the peninsula: Dean was still a category 1 when it exited the far side in 2007.

If you are travelling a long way for a single day between June and November, check the news the evening before. That is the whole precaution.

The equinox is a window, not a date

This is the part that changes plans.

The light serpent on El Castillo — triangles of light descending the north-western balustrade — is sold everywhere as a 20 March event. According to work by four archaeoastronomers, published in Arqueología Mexicana No. 197 and presented by INAH in April 2026, the same seven triangles that form the serpent's complete body appear from 15 to 25 March. The equinox falls exactly in the middle of that stretch, five days either side.

DateWhat is visible

What changes on 15 March is not the count — it is how long the figure holds. In the weeks before, the triangles appear in smaller numbers and, in the authors' words, "disappear before sunset". From 15 March the seven "will remain into the sunsets", through to 25 March. From 26 March an eighth triangle joins and by 9 April there are nine: a longer figure, not the canonical one.

Now the crowd. The spring 2025 operation drew 32,872 people; on 20 March alone, 9,221 came through, and INAH recorded the crowd peaking around 15:30 — exactly when the shadow forms. Spring 2026 drew 24,791 across three days.

So on the most crowded day of the year you compete with nine thousand people for the same twenty minutes, to see what the same pyramid shows on 17 March to a normal day's six thousand.

Three caveats, because this is a single-source claim and a weather-dependent one.

These dates come from one study. The specific window has not been published independently of it, and an earlier paper by overlapping authors describes the seven triangles "around the equinox" without naming dates. The dates are given for 2026 and are tied to calendar intervals built into the pyramid's orientation, which the authors themselves distinguish from the astronomical equinox by about two days — in another year the edges may shift by a day.

The phenomenon needs direct sun between roughly 15:00 and 17:00, and it does fail. At the 2023 spring equinox a frontal system covered the sky and the serpent did not form at all; thousands of people saw nothing. In 2026 cloud nearly took it and the descent appeared late.

Which is the practical argument for the wider window: eleven chances instead of one.

2027: the dates that matter

Spring equinox: Saturday 20 March 2027, 14:25 local time. A Saturday is the worst case — INAH's operation normally covers the Friday to Sunday around the date, so expect heavy crowds from Friday 19 to Sunday 21 March.

Then it gets worse. Mexican school holidays run 22 March to 2 April 2027, with Easter on 28 March. They begin the day after the equinox weekend ends. And Monday 15 March is a national holiday, which is also the first day of the seven-triangle window.

Put together, the March 2027 picture looks like this:

  • 15 March — holiday Monday, first day of the seven triangles
  • 16–18 March — the quiet heart of the window, and our recommendation
  • 19–21 March — the equinox operation, expect the year's heaviest crowds
  • 22 March – 2 April — Mexican school holidays

If you want the serpent without the scrum, Tuesday 17 or Wednesday 18 March 2027 is the answer this page exists to give.

One caveat on the arrangements. INAH publishes its equinox operating plan about a week ahead, so 2027 is not announced yet — in 2026 the gates opened at 09:00 on the equinox Friday, tickets stopped at 16:00, and several monuments closed at 15:30 to funnel people towards the pyramid. There is no booking and no quota: no INAH bulletin has ever set a visitor cap for these days. Chichén Viejo closes, and the evening show is cancelled on part of the weekend.

The best hour, in any month

Gates open at 08:00. INAH's own entry data puts the pressure between 10:00 and 14:00, coaches start arriving around 10:00–11:00, and groups head back to their buses from 14:00. That gives you a clean two hours at the start of the day, every day of the year.

Heat follows the same logic. The hottest hours in Yucatán run from midday into early afternoon, and the state civil-protection advice for the archaeological zones is to start early and avoid long sun exposure. Solar noon at Chichén Itzá falls between 11:39 and 12:09 depending on the month, so the sun is still low when the gates open and most of the walk can be behind you before it is overhead.

For the record, sunrise is between 05:13 (June) and 06:33 (January), and sunset between 17:14 (November) and 18:38 (July). The gates never open at sunrise; 08:00 is as early as the site gets.

One thing we could not establish: whether there is any usable shade on the esplanade and where. No official source describes it, and we are not going to invent one. Every account agrees the site is exposed — plan as if there is none.

What about Sundays and weekends?

Short answer: nobody can say, and that includes us.

Entry is free on Sundays for Mexican nationals and residents, and it is widely repeated that this makes Sunday the busiest day of the week. No published data supports that. INAH's open dataset covers 290 sites from 1996 to 2025 at monthly granularity only; there is no weekday breakdown anywhere, for any site.

The one quantified remark we found — the state heritage body describing "a steady flow of more than 6,000 people every Sunday" — does not settle it either. "More than 6,000" could be 6,100 or 8,300, and a year-round daily average takes no account of season. One reported Sunday in July 2025 came in at 8,300.

So: we do not have the data to say Sunday is the busiest day of the week, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. The only sourced weekday observation we found concerns Holy Week specifically, where the heaviest concentration was reported from Thursday to Sunday. Outside that, choose your day for your own convenience and put the effort into the hour.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best month to visit Chichén Itzá?

November. The rainy season has finished, the average high drops to 31 °C, and the month is never the busiest. December to February have equally good weather but the highest prices and the winter crowds; September and October are far emptier but bring the year's worst rain.

What is the best time of day to visit Chichén Itzá?

08:00, when the gates open, in every month. INAH's entry data puts the pressure between 10:00 and 14:00 and the coaches start arriving around 10:00–11:00, so the first two hours are the quietest of the day — and the coolest, since the hottest hours run from midday into early afternoon.

When is the quietest time to visit Chichén Itzá?

September and October, by a wide margin: around 128,000 visitors against 220,000 in a peak month, two years running. Hotel occupancy near the site drops to 17 per cent in September. The trade-off is the weather — September is the wettest month and the hurricane season peaks on 10 September.

Do I have to go on the equinox to see the serpent?

No. According to the study INAH presented in April 2026, the seven triangles that form the serpent's complete body appear from 15 to 25 March, with the equinox in the middle of that window. On 20 March 2025 some 9,221 people came through in one day, with the crowd peaking at 15:30 — exactly when the figure forms. The phenomenon needs clear sky between about 15:00 and 17:00, so a wider window also means more chances: in 2023 cloud stopped it appearing at all.

When is the spring equinox at Chichén Itzá in 2027?

Saturday 20 March 2027, at 14:25 local time. INAH's operation usually covers the Friday to Sunday around it, so expect heavy crowds from 19 to 21 March. Mexican school holidays then run from 22 March to 2 April, and 15 March is a national holiday.

Do you need to book for the equinox?

No. No INAH bulletin has ever set a visitor cap or a booking requirement for the equinox days. Expect modified hours instead: in 2026 the gates opened at 09:00 on the Friday, ticket sales stopped at 16:00, several monuments closed at 15:30, and Chichén Viejo was shut.

How hot does Chichén Itzá get?

May is the hottest month, averaging 36.2 °C. April 2026 was the fourth-hottest April on record in Mérida at 42.5 °C, with 43 °C in inland municipalities, and Yucatán's record is 49 °C. June to August look milder at 34.5 °C but carry 75 per cent humidity. There is almost no shade on the site.

Does it rain all day in the rainy season?

Usually not. Rain on the peninsula in season is typically convective and afternoon-heavy — CONAGUA's regional forecasts routinely describe storm potential building in the afternoon. An early start often means a dry visit even in September. No one publishes the share of rainfall that falls after midday, so treat this as the pattern rather than a guarantee.

Is Sunday the busiest day at Chichén Itzá?

There is no published weekday data for Chichén Itzá, so nobody can answer that — INAH's open dataset is monthly only. Entry is free on Sundays for Mexican nationals and residents, which is why the claim circulates, but we have not found figures that establish it either way.
