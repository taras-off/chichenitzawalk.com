Chichén Itzá Map: How to Navigate the Archaeological Site

Every official map still shows an entrance that closed in 2026. How the site is actually laid out from the gate you will use, and which distances are real.

By Eugene · updated 21 October 2026 · 11 min read

Start with the thing nobody tells you: every official map of Chichén Itzá shows an entrance you can no longer use.

INAH's own site plan and its printed booklet both put the access, the ticket office and the information point on the west side. Since INAH's statement of 31 May 2026 the only way in is CATVI, the new visitor centre, and it stands about 900 metres north of El Castillo. The approach is longer, it comes from the opposite side, and no up-to-date official map of it has been published by anyone.

The official maps, and what they now get wrong

There are two official maps, and both are worth having. Both are also out of date in the same way.

INAH publishes a site plan — a drawn overhead view with every monument labelled — and a two-page booklet with a simplified map, a lettered key for the seven headline monuments and fifteen numbered minor ones. The booklet is the better one to carry. Its file was made in 2015, under the old CONACULTA branding.

On the site plan, four symbols sit together in one block on the west side: access, parking, toilets and the site museum. There is no ticket office symbol on it at all. On the booklet there are five symbols in two clusters — a western group with access, information and a ticket office, and a second ticket office with access on the south side, towards the hotels.

Neither shows a way in from the north. Neither shows CATVI. On both, the Sacred Cenote sits alone at the top of the map with no services near it, at the end of the causeway the booklet numbers 15, "Sacbé No. 1 and the wall".

That is exactly backwards from what you will do in 2026.

What changed, and what nobody has drawn yet

CATVI — the Centro de Atención a Visitantes — opened its ticket windows on 27 March 2026. It covers 16 hectares and holds the Gran Museo, a car park of 1.72 hectares with 517 spaces, five guide modules, toilets and a craft market with 235 occupied stalls. The Yucatán state government places it "to the north of the pyramid of Kukulkán, about 900 metres away, on the Costera del Golfo federal highway".

On 31 May 2026 INAH stated that "access for the public will be exclusively through the Visitor Attention Centre (Catvi)", and the zone reopened on 1 June after about two weeks closed. Since 19 September 2026 INAH has also been explicit that tickets "can only be bought at the ticket windows of the Catvi".

The old western entrance has not been demolished. INAH and the state still refer to "the former Parador Turístico" and "the old access" as places, most recently in May 2026, when the subject was relocating craft stalls standing between that old access and the UNESCO plaque. What closed is the ticket office, not the building.

So where does the walk now begin? INAH has never described the route in words. What it has said, twice, is that the Gran Museo is entered "from inside the archaeological zone, by the Sacred Cenote area, with the possibility of re-entering the site at the same point", and that on busy days it opens "the exit of the Visitor Attention Centre (by the Sacred Cenote)". The Gran Museo sits inside CATVI. INAH's one map that shows CATVI — in its 2023 Tren Maya guide — draws a pedestrian strip from the CATVI building across the highway to a point beside the Sacred Cenote, joining Sacbé No. 1 down into the Great Plaza.

Put together: you come in from the north, and the Sacred Cenote is on your way in rather than a detour off the Great Plaza. We want to be exact about the status of that: it follows from INAH's own statements and INAH's own map, but INAH has not said it in a sentence, and the map that shows it was drawn in December 2023, before CATVI opened. Treat it as the best available reading, not as a published route.

How INAH actually divides the site

Forget "north group" and "south group". INAH names six groups, and the names are worth knowing because they are what the signs and the official texts use.

GroupWhat is in it
Gran NivelaciónEl Castillo, the Great Ball Court, the Temple of the Warriors, the Temple of the Big Tables, the Tzompantli, the Platform of the Eagles and Jaguars, the Platform of Venus — all on one enormous artificial platform
Grupo de las Mil Columnasthe Thousand Columns colonnades, south-east of the Temple of the Warriors
Grupo del Osarioa secondary plaza linked to the Gran Nivelación by a causeway running south of El Castillo, and east to the Xtoloc cenote by another
Grupo de la Casa Coloradathe Casa Colorada, the Casa del Venado and El Caracol, the observatory
Grupo de las Monjasthe Nunnery, its annex, La Iglesia and an unexcavated ball court
Chichén Viejo / Serie Inicialsouth of the ancient city — a residential group where the elite lived

Two things fall out of that list.

The architecture changes as you walk south. The Great Plaza is built in what INAH calls the Maya-Toltec style. The Nunnery group and Chichén Viejo are Puuc and Chenes — a different tradition, and the reason the southern half of the site looks like it belongs somewhere else. That is not a matter of taste; it is the single most useful orientation fact on the whole site.

The Sacred Cenote is a limb, not a room. It is joined to the Great Plaza by a causeway off the north side, and it sits about 300 metres north of El Castillo at the end of it — the figure comes from Arqueología Mexicana, which also gives the cenote as 60 m across, with the water 22 m below the rim and a maximum depth of 13.5 m.

We tried to measure the gaps off INAH's own site plan, which carries a 200-metre scale bar. It does not survive a check. Measured against its own bar, El Castillo comes out about 89 m across its base, when the real building is about 55 m. The drawing is schematic, the bar is decorative, and any distance table built from it — ours or anyone else's — is fiction. We threw ours away, and we would treat a map page quoting metre-by-metre distances between monuments with suspicion.

What this means in practice: budget by the walk, not by the numbers. The approach from CATVI and back is roughly two kilometres of the day before you have looked at anything, and the monuments themselves add a few more.

What is open and what is not

  • Chichén Viejo / Serie Inicial — INAH's zone page, updated 6 October 2026, lists it as temporarily closed. INAH Lugares still advertises Friday and Saturday slots and tells you to book "at the ticket office of the archaeological zone" — an office that moved to CATVI in March. Do not plan around those slots; ring 985 851 0137 if it matters to you.
  • El Castillo — the cordon around it was widened in April 2025, INAH said, to discourage unauthorised access to the base and to give the custodians a clearer line of sight. You see it from further back than older photographs suggest.
  • The Gran Museo — entered from inside the zone by the Sacred Cenote, and you can come back in at the same point.
  • The Great Ball Court, the Thousand Columns, El Osario, El Caracol, the Nunnery group, the Xtoloc cenote and the Casita Maya — all named in INAH's 2026 operations as open.

Hours, and why two official pages disagree

We have to be careful here, because INAH contradicts itself.

INAH's own zone page, updated 6 October 2026, says: open 08:00 to 17:00, last access 16:00, clearing from 16:30. INAH Lugares, the institute's other portal, says in both Spanish and English: open 08:00 to 16:00, last access 15:00, with priority handling for agencies from 11:00 to 14:00.

Same institute, two pages, an hour apart on both numbers.

Plan on arriving before 15:00. The cost of being wrong is not symmetrical: arrive early on the later schedule and you lose nothing, arrive at 15:30 on the earlier one and you are turned away after a long drive. The same logic applies to the agency priority window on the Lugares page — if it is real, 11:00 to 14:00 is not when you want to be queuing.

For the avoidance of doubt, these are both official pages. This is not a case of an old blog against INAH.

Orientation on the ground: what we could not establish

Four things a map page ought to tell you, which no official source does.

Signage and its languages. Nothing published. INAH's own website having Spanish and English versions tells you nothing about the boards in the field.

Shade. Still unestablished, and we have been looking since we wrote our page on timing. One official document touches it: in May 2026 INAH recorded that the craft sellers' demands included equipping the visitor centre "with telephone, internet and shade services" — which tells you shade at CATVI was short in May 2026, and tells you nothing about the esplanade. Plan as though there is none.

Phone signal and Wi-Fi. Same document, same conclusion: telephone and internet were things CATVI was being asked to provide. Inside the zone there is no published information at all. This is why we say download anything you need before you arrive, rather than claiming the signal is bad.

Toilets, water and food inside the zone. INAH lists toilets, lockers, a cloakroom, an information module, a shop, parking, accessibility and guided visits as site services, with no count and no locations. There is no café or restaurant on that list, and bringing food in is prohibited. Bags and backpacks are searched at the entrance, not banned.

Why the hour and the order beat the map

You can memorise the plan and still have a bad day here, because the two things that decide it are not on any map: when you arrive, and which way round you walk.

Arrive at opening and the Great Plaza is nearly yours. Arrive at eleven and you are sharing it with the coaches that left Cancún at six. Walk the site in the order the buildings were meant to be read — the ceremonial centre first, then the older Puuc architecture to the south — and the place assembles into a city. Wander it by whichever path looks shadiest and it stays a field of impressive rubble.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an official map of Chichén Itzá I can download?

Yes, two, and both predate the current entrance. INAH publishes a drawn site plan and a two-page booklet with a lettered key to the main monuments. Both show the old western access with the ticket office and neither shows CATVI, the visitor centre that INAH has designated the only way in since 31 May 2026. The one official map that does show CATVI is in INAH's 2023 Tren Maya guide, drawn before CATVI opened.

Where is the entrance to Chichén Itzá now?

At CATVI, the visitor centre, which the Yucatán state government places about 900 metres north of the pyramid of Kukulkán on the Costera del Golfo highway. Since 31 May 2026 INAH has stated that public access is exclusively through CATVI, and since September 2026 that tickets can only be bought at its windows. The old western entrance building still stands, but its ticket office is closed.

How far do you walk at Chichén Itzá?

Further than you would guess, because the approach from CATVI to the monuments and back is roughly two kilometres before you have looked at anything, and the site itself adds a few more. Precise distances between monuments are not published by anyone, and INAH's site plan cannot be measured — against its own scale bar El Castillo comes out about 89 m wide when the building is about 55 m.

How is the site laid out?

INAH divides it into six groups rather than halves. The Gran Nivelación is the great artificial platform carrying El Castillo, the Great Ball Court, the Temple of the Warriors, the Tzompantli and the platforms. Then come the Thousand Columns, the Osario group, the Casa Colorada group with the observatory, the Nunnery group, and Chichén Viejo to the south. The architecture changes as you go south, from Maya-Toltec to Puuc and Chenes.

How far is the Sacred Cenote from the pyramid?

About 300 metres north of El Castillo, at the end of Sacbé No. 1, according to Arqueología Mexicana. The cenote is about 60 metres across, with the water some 22 metres below the rim and a maximum depth of 13.5 metres. Because the current entrance is north of the site, the cenote now tends to come early in the visit rather than as a detour from the Great Plaza.

What time does Chichén Itzá open and close?

Two official INAH pages disagree. INAH's zone page, updated 6 October 2026, gives 08:00 to 17:00 with last access at 16:00. INAH Lugares gives 08:00 to 16:00 with last access at 15:00. We recommend arriving before 15:00, because being wrong in that direction costs you nothing and being wrong the other way costs you the visit.

Can you visit Chichén Viejo?

Not reliably. INAH's zone page, updated 6 October 2026, lists the Serie Inicial group as temporarily closed, while INAH Lugares still shows Friday and Saturday time slots and tells you to book at a ticket office that has since moved to CATVI. If it matters to your trip, telephone the zone on 985 851 0137 rather than relying on either page.

Are there toilets, food and water inside the site?

INAH lists toilets, lockers, a cloakroom, an information module, a shop and parking among the site services, but publishes neither how many nor where. There is no café or restaurant on that list, and bringing food into the zone is prohibited. Bags and backpacks are searched at the entrance rather than banned.

Is there shade or phone signal at Chichén Itzá?

Neither is documented. The only official trace we found is from May 2026, when INAH recorded that craft sellers were asking for the visitor centre to be equipped with telephone, internet and shade — which says those were short at CATVI then, and says nothing about conditions inside the zone. Plan for no shade, and download whatever you need before you arrive.
