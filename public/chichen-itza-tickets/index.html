Chichén Itzá Tickets 2026: What You Pay, Where You Pay It and What It Covers

One ticket, 697 pesos, one window inside CATVI. What the price covers, what it does not, and why half the internet still quotes a number that is wrong.

One ticket, one window: what changed in 2026

For years Chichén Itzá charged you twice. There was a federal fee for INAH, the national institute that looks after the archaeology, and a separate Yucatán state fee. Two windows, two queues, two receipts, and a reliable supply of confused visitors who had paid one and not the other.

The Boleto Único ended that. It was trialled at the spring equinox and made permanent on Friday 27 March 2026: one integrated fare, one payment, one window.

The state government's own announcement puts it in exactly those terms — a single payment at a single ticket window. The change landed at the same time as the new CATVI visitor centre on Highway 180, and INAH's regional coordinator described the new arrangement as one ticket office and one access, through CATVI. The old western Parador Turístico stopped selling tickets, barriers went up, and the flow was redirected north.

So if a page tells you to budget for two fees, or warns you about picking the right queue, that page is describing a system that no longer exists.

Two of these rows need the small print spelled out, because the headlines get them wrong.

Yucatán residents are not free. A state decree waives their share of the fare for the whole of 2026, and several outlets ran that as "yucatecos enter free". They do not: the decree only touches the state portion, and the 105-peso federal INAH fee sits in a different law. 105 pesos is what they pay.

The free-entry rules are written for Mexicans. INAH's own information sheet lists the exemptions — under-13s, students and teachers, people with disabilities, INAPAM card holders — under one condition: siempre y cuando sean mexicanos o extranjeros residentes en México. Mexican nationals, or foreigners living in Mexico. A visiting British pensioner with a bus pass gets nothing. Nor does an international student card, as far as any source we can find states.

For a foreign child, the official sources do not give a single clear rate. What is clear is that the under-13 exemption is written for Mexicans and residents of Mexico — INAH's English-language page says so outright. What a foreign child actually pays is not settled: one outlet reports a separate children's rate near 110 pesos, and neither the federal tariff schedule nor the state decree resolves it. Until that is confirmed, budget for a full ticket per child and check the amount at the window.

The price moved twice this year — which is why everyone's numbers are wrong

This is worth a short detour, because it explains the mess.

Every Mexican outlet reporting the 2026 tariffs in early January gave 676 and 303. By 18 February, INAH's own page showed the higher figures. We could not find the instrument that made the change, only the before and the after.

The result is that 676 is still sitting in the headline of travel pages that were genuinely accurate nine months ago. A page that has not been touched since January is not obviously out of date, and that is the trap. When you check a price for Chichén Itzá, check when the page was last updated, not whether it says 2026.

The mechanism behind all this is at least predictable. The federal INAH fee is set through the Ley Federal de Derechos, voted in October and in force from 1 January. The Yucatán state fee is published by decree in the state gazette in late December. So this page has a shelf life: re-check the figure in January 2027, when both instruments land. There is also an initiative to make 53 INAH sites free that year — Chichén Itzá is not among them.

Where you buy it

At the window, inside CATVI. That is the short answer and, as far as anything official says, the only answer.

Online, officially: we could not confirm it is possible. INAH's page for the site links to a ticket portal, ventadeboletosenlinea.inah.gob.mx. The link is generic and the portal shows no inventory without an account, so we could not verify that Chichén Itzá is actually sold there. None of the government announcements about the Boleto Único mention online sales, and a reporter covering the September equinox wrote that tickets were sold at the CATVI windows only. Not being able to confirm a service is not proof it does not exist — but until it is confirmed, the dependable plan is to buy at the CATVI window.

Online, through a reseller: yes, at roughly double. More on that below.

Payment. No official source states which payment methods the CATVI window accepts — not INAH, not the state tourism board, not the press coverage of the new centre. The older guidance circulating online describes the two-window system that no longer exists, and nobody confirms a working cash machine on site. That is not the same as saying cards are refused; it means nobody has published the answer. Bring pesos in cash for the whole amount and treat a card as a bonus if it works.

The queue. INAH's own entry data shows the pressure concentrated between 10:00 and 14:00, and coach groups start arriving around 10:00–11:00. Arrive at opening and the window is quick. Arrive at 11:30 and you are buying a ticket alongside everybody else's day.

What 697 pesos actually gets you

The archaeological zone, all of it: El Castillo, the Great Ball Court, the Temple of the Warriors, the Group of a Thousand Columns, the Sacred Cenote, Cenote Xtoloc, El Osario, El Caracol, Las Monjas and the southern Puuc group. There is no inner ticket, no second gate, no premium section.

Chichén Viejo, the Grupo de la Serie Inicial, which opened in September 2023 and is covered by the ordinary ticket at no extra cost. You cannot simply walk in: it runs as an accompanied visit, and INAH's page gives Fridays and Saturdays, two slots at 09:00–11:30 and 12:00–14:30, groups of 50 maximum, two and a half hours, reserved at the ticket office. That page has not been updated since the entrance moved — it still shows 2025 prices and makes no mention of CATVI — so confirm the current arrangement when you buy. The zone's phone number is 985 851 0137.

And one thing that is not part of your ticket at all, in a good way. The Gran Museo de Chichén Itzá inside CATVI is free to everyone, ticket or no ticket: 3,400 m², 14 thematic sections, over 500 objects, open since February 2024.

Nothing you buy online from anyone includes your admission except an aggregator entry ticket. If you have bought a tour, check whether admission is in the price — on the big platforms a "ticket" is entry only, while a "tour" usually bundles a guide or transport and costs two to four times more.

Skip-the-line tickets: what they actually skip

This is where the money goes missing, so it is worth being precise.

At the window you pay 697 pesos, about US$38. The same entry ticket was US$75 on Viator and US$79 on both GetYourGuide and Tiqets when we checked on 9 October 2026. Reseller prices move faster than official ones, so treat those as a snapshot — but the shape of it, roughly double the window price, has been stable.

Whether you get anything for that depends on the platform, and they do not agree with each other.

  • Tiqets says in its own small print that you still pass the security check and only skip the ticket queue.
  • GetYourGuide claims you can walk past the ticket office entirely and saves up to 45 minutes.
  • Viator sends you a voucher, not a ticket. You exchange it at an operator's desk by the entrance — the listing names a shop — and the actual tickets arrive by WhatsApp or email the day before. That is an extra errand on arrival, which is not what "skip the line" sounds like.

Reviews split the same way. Some travellers report saving real time; others arrived to no queue at all.

How we would decide. If you are at the gate for opening and happy to pay on the spot, the window is almost always the better deal. A reseller ticket earns its premium in high season, on a tight schedule, or when having the purchase settled in advance is worth money to you in itself. Whichever you choose, read what the listing actually promises to skip: the ticket queue, the collection of a voucher, or something else. Security screening usually applies either way.

Three ways to buy the wrong thing

Check the price and the date together. The commonest problem by far, and nobody is lying to you — the pages are just old. A figure of 676 was accurate in January. Look for when the page was last updated, not for the year in the headline.

Know whether you are buying a ticket or a voucher. Some resellers send a document you still have to exchange on site, at a desk, before you can enter. Find that out at the checkout, not in the car park.

Buy the night show only through the official channel. The state tourism body has taken legal action over counterfeit Noches de Kukulkán tickets. The day ticket has no equivalent documented problem — we found no reported case of fake day tickets sold at the entrance — but the night show does, and it is sold online.

One thing that is not a scam and will ruin a day anyway: the zone closed for about two weeks in May 2026 during a dispute with the Pisté artisans, reopening on 1 June. It is rare, but if you are travelling a long way for a single day, glance at the news the evening before.

Hours, validity and the discrepancy in INAH's own pages

08:00 to 16:00, every day. That part is not in dispute.

The last entry is. INAH's Spanish page for the site gives 08:00–16:00 without naming a cut-off. INAH's English page for the same site says last access is 15:00. INAH's own bulletins, including the operational notice for the September 2026 equinox, describe ticket sales and entry running until 16:00. The English page also still shows 2025 state fees, which tells you how recently it was reviewed — but that is a reason to doubt it, not to dismiss it.

So: arrive before 15:00. When official sources disagree about a cut-off, the later figure is the one you cannot afford to be wrong about, and arriving at 15:30 on the strength of it means a locked gate and a wasted drive from Cancún. For a visit worth making, come several hours earlier than that — and if a late arrival is unavoidable, call 985 851 0137 first.

Validity. No official source states whether the ticket is valid for one day only, or whether you may leave and re-enter. We looked. Assume one entry, one day, and ask at the window if it matters to your plan.

What the ticket does not buy

You now know what Chichén Itzá costs and where to pay it. What is left is the thing a ticket has never covered.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a ticket to Chichén Itzá cost in 2026?

697 pesos for a foreign adult, about €34 or US$38 — one Boleto Único, paid at one window inside the CATVI visitor centre. Mexican nationals pay 310 pesos with ID and Yucatán residents 105 pesos. The price rose twice during 2026, so a page quoting 676 or 648 is out of date rather than wrong about a different ticket.

Can I buy Chichén Itzá tickets online?

We could not confirm that you can, officially. INAH's page links to a ticket portal, but it shows no inventory without an account, no government announcement about the Boleto Único mentions online sales, and recent reporting describes tickets sold at the CATVI windows only. Resellers like Tiqets, GetYourGuide and Viator do sell entry tickets online, at roughly double the window price.

Is there a skip-the-line ticket for Chichén Itzá?

Resellers sell one, at around US$75–79 against about US$38 at the window, and the platforms describe it differently: Tiqets says you skip only the ticket queue and still clear security, GetYourGuide says you bypass the ticket office entirely, and Viator sends a voucher you exchange at a desk near the entrance. It earns its premium in high season or on a tight schedule. If you arrive at opening and are happy to pay on the spot, the window is usually the better deal.

Do children pay at Chichén Itzá?

Foreign children do. INAH writes its free entry for under-13s for Mexicans and residents of Mexico, and its English-language page says so explicitly. The exact amount a foreign child pays is not settled in the official sources, so budget for a full ticket per child and check at the window.

Do Yucatán residents enter free?

No — they pay 105 pesos, the federal INAH fee. A state decree waives the Yucatán portion for 2026, which several headlines turned into "free entry", but the federal fee comes from a different law and still applies.

Is Chichén Itzá free on Sundays?

For Mexican nationals and foreigners resident in Mexico, with ID — not for visiting tourists. It can also mean more people at the gate, so if your dates are flexible, consider a weekday.

What is included in the Chichén Itzá entrance fee?

The whole archaeological zone, and Chichén Viejo as an accompanied visit by reservation. The Gran Museo de Chichén Itzá inside CATVI is free to everyone, with or without a ticket. Parking, lockers, a guide, the Noches de Kukulkán night show and any audio guide are all extra.

Can I pay by card at Chichén Itzá?

No official source says which cards the CATVI window accepts, and none confirms a working cash machine on site. That is a gap in the published information rather than a refusal, but it means cash in pesos is the only reliable plan.

What time do tickets stop being sold?

The site runs 08:00–16:00, but official sources disagree on the cut-off for entry: INAH's English page says 15:00 while its bulletins describe entry until 16:00. Arrive before 15:00 to be safe, and call 985 851 0137 if your whole day depends on a late arrival.
